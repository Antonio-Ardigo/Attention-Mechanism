Attention, worked out

The Math Behind Attention

Queries, keys, and values are three matrices doing one job: deciding which words should listen to which, and what they hear when they do. Here is the whole mechanism, one step at a time, with every step you can actually play with.

This walkthrough follows the line of Luis Serrano's “The math behind Attention: Keys, Queries, and Values matrices” (Serrano.Academy, ~33 min) — same order, same worked example. Quotations are his. The interactive figures, the shape tables, and the closing list of what the video leaves out are additions.

Attention is built on one idea: similarity. Everything else — the softmax, the three matrices, the square root, the multiple heads — is machinery for measuring similarity in a space where measuring it is worth doing.

Hold onto that and the formula stops being a wall of symbols. It becomes a sentence you can read left to right.

Step 01 · The problemTwo sentences, one word

Attention exists because a word's meaning is not in the word.

A model turns each word into a vector — a list of numbers that places it somewhere in a high-dimensional space, near words it resembles. That works beautifully until a word resembles two unrelated things at once:

“an apple and orange”  ·  “an apple phone”

Same word. In the first sentence it belongs beside pear and banana. In the second it belongs beside Samsung and laptop. A single fixed vector for apple has to sit somewhere between the fruit neighbourhood and the technology neighbourhood — which means it is wrong in both sentences.

So the vector has to move, and what moves it has to be the surrounding words. That is the entire job description. Everything from here is how it gets done.

Step 02 · SimilarityThree ways to ask “how close?”

Before words can pull on each other, we need a number for how related two of them are.

There are three candidates, and they build on each other.

Dot product. Multiply the vectors coordinate by coordinate and add up the results. If one coordinate means “fruitness” and another means “techness”, then two fruit words each have a big first coordinate, their product is big, and the sum comes out high. Two unrelated words have their large coordinates in different places, so nothing lines up and the total stays small.

“…when we add them together — that's the product of the tech and the product of the fruitness — we get a high number.”

Serrano, on why the dot product measures relatedness

Cosine. The dot product has a flaw: it grows when vectors are simply longer, regardless of direction. Divide it by both lengths and what's left is purely the angle — from 1 (pointing the same way) through 0 (perpendicular, unrelated) to −1 (opposite).

Scaled dot product. Cosine is well-behaved but throws away magnitude entirely, and normalising every pair is expensive. The compromise attention actually uses is to keep the dot product and divide by √d, the square root of the number of dimensions. Drag the vectors below and watch all three at once.

Why √d specifically? Because dot products are sums over dimensions. Add more dimensions and you add more terms, so the totals spread out — with a thousand coordinates the numbers get big enough to cause trouble downstream.

“When you have very long vectors — for example with a thousand coordinates — you get really, really large dot products, and you don't want that. You want to manage these numbers.”

Serrano, on the scaling term

The trouble is specific, and it's worth naming because the video doesn't: the next step is a softmax. Feed a softmax numbers that are far apart and it returns something close to all-or-nothing — one weight near 1, the rest near 0. A function pinned at its extremes has almost no slope, and no slope means no gradient, and no gradient means the layer stops learning. Dividing by √d keeps the scores in the range where softmax still has something to say.

Step 03 · The gridEvery pair at once

One similarity is a number. All of them together are a table.

A sentence of n words gives n × n pairs, and we want the similarity of every one of them — including each word with itself, which is always the highest score in its row, since nothing resembles a word more than the word does.

Read the grid a row at a time. Row apple answers: of everything in this sentence, what does apple resemble? That row is about to become the recipe for apple's new vector.

Step 04 · SoftmaxTurning closeness into shares

Raw similarities can't be used as weights. They have to be made into a set of proportions first.

We want each row of the grid to become a blend: 60% of this word, 30% of that one, 10% of the rest. That means the row has to be all-positive and add to exactly 1. Cosine scores are neither — some are negative, and they add to whatever they add to.

The fix is softmax, and it is two moves. First raise e to the power of each score, which forces every value positive while keeping the order intact — a bigger score is still a bigger number afterwards. Then divide each one by the total, which makes them sum to 1.

The exponential is not just a way to remove minus signs. It exaggerates: it widens the gap between the top score and the rest, so a word that is somewhat more relevant ends up considerably more influential. How much it exaggerates is exactly what the √d divisor controls.

Step 05 · AttentionWords move toward their context

With a row of weights in hand, the mechanism does the obvious thing: it takes a weighted average.

Apple's new vector is every word's vector, each multiplied by apple's weight for it, all added up. Words it scored highly contribute most; words it ignored barely register.

Geometrically this is a word being dragged toward the company it keeps. Put orange in the sentence and apple slides into the fruit neighbourhood. Put phone there instead and the same starting vector slides the other way. Nothing about apple changed — only who else was present.

This is real attention, and it already solves the problem from step one. But it has a weakness that motivates everything left in the video: it uses the plain embedding space for two jobs at once — deciding who attends to whom, and supplying what gets passed along. Those two jobs want different geometry.

Step 06 · K and QKeys and queries: a space for matching

The first fix: don't measure similarity in the original space. Measure it in one built for the purpose.

The embedding space a model starts with was shaped by general word meaning. It is not necessarily the space where the distinctions that matter for attention are the easy ones to see. So before comparing anything, multiply every vector by a matrix — a linear transformation that stretches, rotates, and squashes the space into a more useful arrangement.

“…transform our embedding into one that is better suited for this attention problem.”

Serrano, on what the keys and queries matrices do

There are two such matrices, not one, because the comparison is directional. When a word looks around, it is asking a question — that projection is its query. When it is being looked at, it is offering a description of itself — that projection is its key. What a word wants and what a word advertises are different things, so they get different matrices.

Every score in the grid is now a query dotted with a key, in the new space, rather than two raw embeddings dotted together.

A common wrong turn: aren't Q and K transposes of each other?

No — and the confusion is worth clearing up carefully, because the truth underneath it is the most important property attention has.

The transpose in QKᵀ is shape bookkeeping. It turns the key matrix on its side so the multiplication pairs every query with every key in one go. It says nothing about a relationship between WQ and WK, which are two independent matrices, separately initialised and separately trained.

Now write out the score for word i attending to word j, and something interesting falls out:

score(i, j) = (xiWQ) · (xjWK)ᵀ = xi (WQWKᵀ) xjᵀ

The two matrices only ever appear as the single product M = WQWKᵀ. And because they are independent, M is not symmetric — which means score(i, j) ≠ score(j, i). How much apple attends to orange is a genuinely different number from how much orange attends to apple.

That asymmetry is the whole point. It is what lets a verb seek its subject without the subject being obliged to seek the verb back — what makes attention directional rather than merely mutual.

And here is what makes the misconception load-bearing: if the two matrices really were transposes of each other, then M = WQWQᵀ, which is symmetric by construction. Every score would equal its mirror. Direction would vanish. Tying them together doesn't create the query/key distinction — it destroys it.

Step 07 · VValues: a space for predicting

The second fix, and the sharpest point in the whole video.

We now have a space arranged for comparing words. Here's the catch: that is a terrible place to leave them. It was optimised for matching, and matching is not what the rest of the model needs. What the rest of the model needs is a representation that helps predict the next word.

So there is a third matrix. Once the weights have been computed in the matching space, the weighted average is taken over value vectors — the same words projected through a different matrix, into a space arranged for the downstream task.

“The embedding on the left is not where you want to move the words — you only want it for calculating similarities. The embedding on the right is optimized for finding the next word in a sentence.”

Serrano, on why the values matrix is separate

Two spaces, two purposes. Q and K decide how much; V decides what. Collapse them into one matrix and you force a single arrangement of the space to be simultaneously good at finding relevant words and good at representing them for prediction. Keeping them apart is what lets each be good at its own job.

Step 08 · AssemblyThe formula, assembled

Every symbol below is a step you have already seen.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √dk ) V

Read it right to left inside the brackets, then out:

PieceShapeWhat just happened
Xn × dmodelThe sentence: one embedding row per word.
Q = X·WQn × dkEvery word's question, in the matching space.
K = X·WKn × dkEvery word's advertisement, same space.
V = X·WVn × dvEvery word's contribution, in the prediction space.
QKᵀn × nThe grid from step three — every query against every key.
÷ √dkn × nScores brought back into a range softmax can work with.
softmax(·)n × nEach row becomes positive shares that sum to 1.
(·)Vn × dvThe weighted average. One new vector per word.

The output has one row per word, exactly like the input — which is what allows these blocks to be stacked, each one re-asking the question in a space the previous layer has already improved.

Notation

The video writes this product as K·Qᵀ; the paper and every implementation write Q·Kᵀ. It is the same computation transposed, but if you go from the video to code expecting the video's arrangement, your dimensions will not line up. Use Q·Kᵀ — rows are queries, columns are keys.

Step 09 · Multi-headMany heads at once

One set of matrices asks one kind of question. Models run several sets in parallel.

Reading the word were, you might want to know what its subject is, what tense the sentence is in, and whether you are inside a quotation. A single Q/K/V triple produces one attention pattern, so it can chase one of those. The answer is to run many triples side by side — each a head, each with its own three matrices.

Their outputs are then glued together end to end, and one final matrix squeezes the result back down to the size the rest of the model expects. That last matrix is not bookkeeping: it is where the model learns how much to trust each head.

“Concatenate them first… a linear step over here is a rectangular matrix that is going to transform this into a lower dimensional embedding.”

Serrano, on combining heads

Because each head starts from its own random matrices, they diverge during training and end up doing recognisably different jobs — one tracking the previous word, another linking pronouns to names, another spotting repeated patterns. Nobody assigns those jobs.

Step 10 · OriginWhere the matrices come from

Every matrix on this page is learned. None of them is designed.

It would be reasonable to assume, after ten steps of careful construction, that someone sat down and worked out what WQ, WK, and WV should contain. Nobody did. They start as small random numbers.

What happens instead is that the model is asked, over and over, to predict the next word. When it gets one wrong, the error is traced back through every operation that contributed — including the attention weights — and each matrix is nudged fractionally in the direction that would have made the prediction less wrong. Repeat across billions of examples.

No training example contains an attention pattern. There is no label saying this verb should look at that subject. The grammar-shaped behaviour that appears in trained models appears because it happens to reduce next-word error, and for no other reason. The three matrices are not instructions; they are residue.

That is also why a trained model handles sentences it has never seen. What was learned is a general way of asking and advertising, not a stored list of which words go with which.

Step 11 · DeltasWhat the video leaves out

The simplifications are the right ones for a first pass. They are still simplifications, and each one bites eventually.

  • Position is nowhere. Everything above is order-blind — shuffle the sentence and the arithmetic gives the same answer. Real transformers add positional information to the embeddings before attention runs, which is the only reason “dog bites man” differs from “man bites dog”.
  • No causal mask. Here every word sees every other word. A model generating text must not see the future, so the scores above the diagonal are set to −∞ before the softmax, making their weights exactly zero.
  • “Moving words through space” is a metaphor. A useful one, and the figures above lean on it — but the operation is a weighted sum of value vectors, and the result lives in the value space, not in the space the words started in. Do not expect to find the original word anywhere near where it appears to land.
  • The block isn't just attention. Around every attention layer sits a residual connection, a normalisation step, and a feed-forward network. Remove them and deep transformers do not train at all.
  • Attention weights are not an explanation. A heat map shows what each word looked at. That is genuinely informative and it is not the same as knowing why the model produced its answer — information also flows along paths the weights don't display.
