How Query Key Value roles emerge

A complete, self-contained explainer — from “what is a word vector” all the way to “why nobody had to assign the query, key, and value jobs.” Built for smart people who are not mathematicians.

No prior ML required. Every symbol is defined before it is used. Four interactive widgets, one runnable Python script. Companion to the training plans in this repo.

The one claim this page defends: the three roles — query, key, value — are not designed. Three matrices that begin nearly identical get pulled into different jobs by differently-shaped gradients, and they only specialize when the task actually rewards asymmetry. Everything below builds the vocabulary and the mechanism to make that sentence obvious.

1The problem attention solves

Before any matrices, the motivation — because the roles only make sense once you know what job they are competing to do.

Read these two sentences:

“I deposited the cheque at the bank.”   vs.   “We had a picnic on the bank of the river.”

The word bank is spelled identically both times, yet it means two unrelated things. Nothing inside the word tells you which. The meaning lives in the company the word keeps — cheque pulls it one way, river the other. A language model that treats each word as a fixed dictionary entry is stuck: it has one “bank” and no way to bend it toward the right sense.

Intuition So the core requirement is: let every word look at every other word and pull in the information that is relevant to it. “bank” should be able to reach out, notice “cheque” is nearby and relevant, and absorb some of its meaning. That reaching-out-and-absorbing operation is exactly what attention is.

Earlier approaches struggled here. Bag-of-words models threw away order entirely. Fixed windows (n-grams) could only see a few neighbours. Recurrent networks passed information along a chain, so a link between word 1 and word 20 had to survive nineteen hops of a game of telephone. Attention’s move is radical and simple: connect every word to every other word directly, in one step, weighted by relevance. The rest of this page is about the machinery that computes those weights — and how that machinery teaches itself what “relevant” means.

Where this is going To weigh relevance, attention needs each word to do three separable things: ask what it is looking for, advertise what it offers, and hand over its content. Those are the query, key, and value. Next we define them precisely — then we watch them arise on their own.

2Definitions: x, W, and the three projections

Every symbol used on this page, defined once, in order. Nothing here is advanced — it is just careful.

Definition · token & embedding A token is roughly a word (sometimes a word-piece). A model cannot do arithmetic on letters, so each token is turned into a vector — a fixed-length list of numbers — called its embedding, written x. Think of x as coordinates that place the word in a “meaning space,” where similar words sit near each other. On this page we use tiny 2-number vectors like x = [1, 0] so we can see everything; real models use hundreds or thousands of numbers.
Definition · weight matrix A weight matrix W is a grid of numbers that transforms one vector into another: you feed in x and get out a new vector. These grids are the learned part of the model — “learning” literally means adjusting the numbers inside the Ws. There are three of them in an attention layer.
Definition · the three projections Q, K, V Each token’s embedding x is pushed through three different weight matrices to produce three different vectors:
Q = x · WQ   the query — “what am I looking for?”
K = x · WK   the key   — “what do I advertise to others?”
V = x · WV   the value — “what content do I hand over if chosen?”
The word projection just means “reshaped by a matrix.” Q, K, V are three reshapings of the same word.

Query — the search

Q = x·WQ
the question a word asks of every other word

Key — the label

K = x·WK
the tagline a word offers to be matched against

Value — the payload

V = x·WV
the information a word delivers when attended to
Intuition · the library analogy You walk into a library with a query (“books about volcanoes”). Every book has a key printed on its spine (its title/subject). You compare your query to the spines to decide relevance. Then you actually read the value — the contents inside. Crucially, the thing you match on (the spine) is separate from the thing you receive (the contents). Attention keeps key and value separate for exactly this reason.
Pitfall to avoid Q, K, and V are not three different words. They are three views of the same token. And nobody hand-writes “this matrix is the query” — the labels are just names we give to three matrices after training has made them behave differently. Holding onto “they’re designed roles” is the single biggest obstacle to understanding emergence.

3What is S? The score matrix

This is the pivot of the whole story, so we define it slowly and then compute a real one.

Definition · the score S Once every token has a query and a key, we ask: how well does token i’s query match token j’s key? The answer is a single number, their dot product:
Sij = Qi · Kj = Qi[1]·Kj[1] + Qi[2]·Kj[2] + …
Collect all those numbers for every pair (i, j) and you get a square grid S — the score matrix. If there are n tokens, S is n×n. Row i is “how much token i (as a seeker) is drawn to each other token.”
Definition · the dot product, in one line The dot product of two vectors multiplies them entry-by-entry and adds the results. It is big when the two vectors point the same way, near zero when they are unrelated, negative when they oppose. So Sij is literally a similarity meter between a query and a key: high score = “these two are a good match.”
Worked example · one cell of S by hand Take three tokens — the, cat, sat — with these tiny 2-D vectors:
Queries  Qthe=[1.0, 0.2]   Qcat=[0.2, 1.0]   Qsat=[0.9, 0.4]
Keys     Kthe=[1.0, 0.1]   Kcat=[0.1, 1.0]   Ksat=[0.8, 0.3]
The score of the looking at the:
Sthe,the = Qthe·Kthe = 1.0×1.0 + 0.2×0.1 = 1.02
The score of cat looking at the:
Scat,the = 0.2×1.0 + 1.0×0.1 = 0.30
Do this for all nine pairs and you get the full grid on the left below.
Definition · from scores to attention weights (softmax) Raw scores can be any size, and we want each word’s attention to be a set of shares that add up to 100%. The softmax function does exactly that to each row: it makes every number positive and rescales them to sum to 1, while exaggerating the largest (winner-takes-more, not winner-takes-all). The result is A = softmax(S), the attention weight matrix. (Real models first divide S by √(vector length) to keep the numbers from getting huge — a detail, not a concept.)

Here is the actual computation for our three words. Left: the score matrix S. Right: the attention weights A = softmax(S), each row summing to 1. Brighter = larger.

S = Q·Kᵀ  (raw scores)
A = softmax(S)  (rows sum to 1)

Read a row left-to-right: it is one word’s distribution of attention over all words. Notice each word attends most to itself here (the diagonal is bright) — an artefact of these particular numbers, not a law. Change the vectors and the pattern changes; that malleability is the whole point.

Definition · the output O Finally, each token builds its new representation by taking a weighted blend of everyone’s values, using its row of attention weights:
Oi = Ai,the·Vthe + Ai,cat·Vcat + Ai,sat·Vsat
Oi is the context-aware version of token i — it has literally absorbed a bit of the words it found relevant. This is where “bank” becomes river-bank or money-bank.
The one equation, assembled
Attention = softmax( QKᵀ / √d ) V
Every piece now has a name: Q and K make the scores S, softmax turns them into weights A, and those weights blend the Values into the output. Q and K decide where to look; V decides what gets delivered. Keep that split in mind — it is the reason the three roles are trained by different signals.

4The forward pass, as a five-step recipe

Nothing new here — just the pipeline in order, so the moving parts are fixed in place before we ask how they’re learned.

StepOperationPlain-English meaning
1Q,K,V = xWQ, xWK, xWVGive every word three roles by reshaping it three ways.
2S = Q·KᵀScore every query against every key — the similarity grid.
3A = softmax(S/√d)Turn each row of scores into shares that add to 100%.
4O = A·VBlend the values by those shares — context flows in.
5compare O to the goal → lossGrade the result against “predict the next word.”
The hinge Steps 1–4 are the forward pass: what attention does. Step 5 produces a loss — a single number measuring how wrong the prediction was. Everything from here on is about how that one number reaches back through steps 4→1 and edits the three W matrices. That backward journey is where the roles are born.

5The five core principles

The emergence of Q/K/V rests on five ideas. We build them in dependency order — each one sets up the next.

#PrincipleIn one line
P1Learned projectionsQ/K/V are three learned matrices, not designed roles.
P3Gradient = blameEach weight is nudged opposite its share of the error.
P2Symmetry breakingIdentical things change identically — until something breaks the tie.
P4Positional differentiationQ, K, V sit in different spots, so they get different gradients.
P5Emergent rolesThe roles are the optimizer’s solution to one objective.

(We teach P3 before P2 because “identical gradients” only makes sense once “gradient” is defined.)

P1Learned projections — the roles are numbers, not labels

Principle Q, K, and V come from three separate learned weight matrices applied to the same token. No role is hand-assigned; “query/key/value” are names for what the trained numbers end up doing.
Worked example Same word x = [1, 0], three random matrices, three different outputs:
WQ=[[0.9, 0.1],[0.2, 0.8]] → Q = [0.9, 0.1]
WK=[[0.3, 0.7],[0.6, 0.4]] → K = [0.3, 0.7]
WV=[[0.5, 0.5],[0.1, 0.9]] → V = [0.5, 0.5]
The word never changed. The three outputs differ only because the three grids of numbers differ. Those grids are the entire learned content of the layer.
Intuition Picture three tinted lenses laid over the same photograph. The photo (the word) is fixed; each lens brings out different features. Training is the slow grinding of those lenses. At the start they are near-identical blanks; by the end one is shaped for “asking,” one for “advertising,” one for “delivering.” Nobody engraved the labels — the grinding did.
Logical connection → P3 If the roles are just numbers being ground into shape, the next question is: what does the grinding? What force decides which way each number moves? That force is the gradient. On to P3.

P3Gradient = blame — how any weight learns

Principle Training nudges every weight in the direction that reduces the error, by an amount proportional to how much that weight contributed to the error. Formally, w ← w − η · (∂L/∂w).
Definition · gradient, without calculus The gradient ∂L/∂w answers one question: “if I nudge this weight up a hair, does the error go up or down, and how fast?” It is the slope of error-versus-weight. Positive slope → raising w makes things worse → so we lower w. That is all “take the gradient and step against it” means: walk downhill on the error.
Worked example · one weight, one step A weight w = 0.5. We find that nudging it up makes the error worse, so its gradient is +2. Learning rate η = 0.1.
w ← 0.5 − 0.1 × (+2) = 0.3
We moved against the slope, and the size of the move (0.2) is set by how steep the blame was. Repeat this for every weight, millions of times, and the network descends toward low error. “Learning” is just this subtraction, run at enormous scale.
Intuition Imagine the error as a hilly landscape and each weight as a knob that slides you around on it. The gradient is the direction of steepest uphill. You want the valley (low error), so you always step the opposite way. A weight that barely affects the error has a near-zero gradient and barely moves — it isn’t to blame, so it isn’t corrected.
Pitfall The minus sign is load-bearing. Add the gradient instead of subtracting it and you climb toward maximum error. “Gradient descent” is the descent; the gradient itself points the wrong way on purpose.
Logical connection → P2 Now we can state the trap precisely. If two weights are identical and the error blames them identically, they receive the same nudge — so they stay identical forever. That is the symmetry problem. On to P2.

P2Symmetry breaking — why twins must be pried apart

Principle Identical parameters change identically as long as the pressure on them is identical. To make two matrices become different, something must break the symmetry — either a different starting point, or an objective that pushes them unequally.
Worked example · the twins that never separate Two weights start equal, wQ = wK = 0.5, and (because they sit symmetrically) always receive the same gradient g each step.
step 1:  both → 0.5 − η·g  → still equal
step 2:  both → (same) − η·g'  → still equal
… 1,000,000 steps later:  still exactly equal
The update is deterministic: same value in, same gradient, same value out. Sameness is a fixed point — the training can never escape it on its own.
Intuition · two ways to break a tie There are exactly two escapes from the twin trap:
  1. Different starts. Initialize the matrices with different random numbers. Now their gradients differ from step one and they drift apart. (This is why “initialize to all zeros” is a classic bug — the twins never separate.)
  2. An unequal objective. Even from an identical start, if the task pushes the query direction and the key direction differently, their gradients differ and they separate. The experiment in P5 isolates exactly this route.
Pitfall “Symmetry” here is not vague. It is the precise fact that swapping the two matrices leaves the situation unchanged. As long as that swap-symmetry holds, the twins are welded together. Breaking the symmetry — in the parameters or in the objective — is a prerequisite for specialization, not a nice-to-have.
Logical connection → P4 So the question becomes concrete: does the attention mechanism give Q and K different gradients? If yes, symmetry breaks and roles can form. The answer is yes, and the reason is purely structural — it comes from where each matrix sits in the computation. That is P4, and it is the heart of the whole story.

P4Positional differentiation — the engine of specialization

Principle Q, K, and V occupy different positions in the computation, so the chain rule delivers them structurally different gradients. Q and K meet at the score, so each one’s correction is scaled by the other; V is reached only through the output, so it learns from a different signal entirely.

Recall the score of a match: s = Q·K. When training wants to change that score, it must decide how to adjust Q and K. Because they are multiplied together, the correction to each depends on the other. Play with it — set the query, the key, and the error signal on the score, and watch the two gradients:

score  s = Q·K = 12
learning rate η = 0.1
gradient to Q = ∂L/∂s × K
= -1 × 4 = -4
gradient to K = ∂L/∂s × Q
= -1 × 3 = -3

Why ∂L/∂Q ∝ K (the one bit of chain rule, gently) The score is s = Q·K. Ask: “if I nudge Q up a little, how much does s move?” It moves by K — because Q is multiplied by K. So Q’s influence on the loss is (∂L/∂s) × K. By the identical argument, K’s influence is (∂L/∂s) × Q. Same-shaped formula; different multiplier. Since Q ≠ K in general, the two corrections are different numbers — and that difference is what pulls them apart.
Reading the widget With Q=3, K=4, ∂L/∂s=−1: Q’s nudge is −1×4 = −4, K’s is −1×3 = −3. Symmetric in shape, different in value. Set Q = K and the two nudges become equal — the twin trap of P2, live. Any gap between Q and K, and they drift further apart each step.
And V is different still The value matrix never appears in the score s. Its correction arrives through the output blend O = A·V, scaled by the attention weight A the word received: ∂L/∂V ∝ A. So V learns from “how the delivered content affected the answer,” while Q and K learn from “how the routing affected the answer.” Two circuits, two signals — routing (Q, K) and content (V) — which is why all three specialize into different jobs.

6The gradient map

One picture of everything in P4. Forward pass flows down; the learning signal flows back up the dashed arrows. Follow where each correction comes from.

Q and K meet at the score, so each one’s correction carries the other’s value — symmetric in shape, different in number. V never touches the score; it is corrected only through the output, in proportion to the attention it won. Three positions, three signals, three roles.

P5Emergent roles — watch them appear

Principle The meaning of “query,” “key,” and “value” is a side effect of minimizing the training loss. The optimizer is not told the roles; it discovers that splitting labour this way is what lowers the error.

Time to prove it. Below is a genuinely-trained toy: two tokens, 2-D lenses, plain gradient descent. Both matrices start identical (so we are testing route #2 of P2 — symmetry broken by the objective, not by the initialization). We plot how far apart the query and key matrices drift, ‖WQ − WK‖, over training. The task decides whether they specialize:

  • Directional task — token 0 must attend to token 1, but token 1 must not attend back. Asymmetric. A single shared lens cannot express “A→B but not B→A.”
  • Symmetric task — mutual matching, A↔B equal. A shared lens handles it perfectly, so there is no pressure to specialize.
Directional task — 0 attends 1, not the reverse Symmetric task — mutual matching
What you just saw Same architecture, same identical start, same optimizer — only the objective differs. The directional task pries the two lenses apart (the curve climbs to ≈2.4): query and key become genuinely different matrices. The symmetric task leaves them welded (the curve stays at 0): a shared lens already solves it, so the gradients on the two never differ. The roles emerged only when the task rewarded them.
Why this settles the “who assigned the roles?” question Nobody edited the two matrices toward different jobs. We handed the optimizer one goal and let gradient descent run. Specialization fell out of minimizing the loss on a directional task — it is the cheapest way to be right. That is what “emergent” means: not random, not designed, but the forced consequence of an objective under pressure.
What the graph does NOT prove A rising curve shows the two lenses became different — it does not show that a real model’s head learned any specific human concept like “grammar” or “coreference.” Those semantic claims require probing the model with designed inputs and reading its attention, not staring at a divergence line. Emergence explains that roles form; it does not, by itself, name them.

7The whole argument in one chain

Every link is something established above. Read top to bottom and the conclusion is unavoidable.

  1. Q, K, V are just three matrices of numbers applied to the same word (P1). The “roles” are names for behaviour, not settings.
  2. Numbers are learned by gradient descent — nudged against the slope of the error, in proportion to blame (P3).
  3. Identical matrices under identical pressure stay identical forever (P2). To specialize, the symmetry must break.
  4. The mechanism breaks it structurally: Q and K meet at the score, so each gets a gradient scaled by the other; V is reached only through the output (P4). Their gradients differ by position.
  5. Under a task that rewards asymmetry, those different gradients drive the matrices apart — and we watched it happen (P5).
  6. Therefore the query/key/value roles are emergent: the optimizer’s discovered solution to one objective — “predict the next word” — not an assignment any engineer made.
Corollary This is also why independently trained models keep discovering the same roles nobody coordinated. Same objective + same statistics of language → the same low-error solution is found again and again. Convergent structure under shared pressure is the fingerprint of emergence.

8Where the Q/K/V idea comes from

The names are borrowed, not invented — and the mechanism was engineered to kill a bottleneck, not derived from a theorem.

The source: a soft dictionary lookup Query / key / value come straight from information retrieval and key-value databases.
Hard lookup (a Python dict):
value = table[key]
one key, exact match, one value
Soft lookup (attention):
compare query to all keys → weights → blend of values
every key, fuzzy match, weighted blend — and differentiable, so it can be learned
The library analogy, restated You bring a query ("books on volcanoes"), scan the keys on the spines, and read the values inside the best matches. Attention is that, made continuous: instead of picking one book you take a weighted blend of all of them, heavier on the closer matches.
The lineage (short and honest)
  • Content-addressable / associative memory — retrieve by what something is, not where it is stored.
  • Memory Networks & Neural Turing Machines (2014–15) — differentiable memory addressed by content; explicit keys and values.
  • Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2014) — attention for translation ("align & translate"); Luong et al. (2015) — the dot-product form.
  • Vaswani et al. (2017), "Attention Is All You Need" — names Query/Key/Value and scaled dot-product attention, framed as "mapping a query and a set of key–value pairs to an output."
"Borrowed post hoc" — the abstraction came after the mechanism The Query/Key/Value vocabulary did not invent attention; it was laid over a mechanism that had already grown, messily, elsewhere. The order of events:
  • Attention first, without the names (2014). Bahdanau's translation model scored a decoder state against each encoder vector h_j, softmaxed the scores, and summed the same h_j's. Notice: h_j was both the thing matched against and the thing summed — key and value were the same vector. There was no "query/key/value" — just "align, then average."
  • Memory networks split key from value (2014–16). End-to-End Memory Networks (Sukhbaatar 2015) stored each fact twice: an input embedding m_i used to match the question, and a separate output embedding c_i used to form the answer. That is exactly key vs value, addressed by a question vector u = the query. Key-Value Memory Networks (Miller 2016) then made the split explicit and named it. Neural Turing Machines (Graves 2014) had already used content-based addressing — a key compared to memory by similarity to produce read-weights.
  • The Transformer unified and renamed it (2017). Vaswani et al. took these scattered ingredients — a query, content-addressed keys, separate values — and compressed them into one crisp formula, softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V, then did the genuinely new thing: made Q, K, V three learned linear projections of the same sequence, so a sequence could attend to itself (self-attention). The database metaphor — "a query retrieving from a key-value store" — was already the mental model in memory networks; the Transformer simply adopted its clean nouns.
So "post hoc" means: the computation was discovered by engineering (fix translation → separate match-from-content → drop recurrence), and only afterwards was it wrapped in the tidy retrieval story that now makes it feel inevitable. The names are a lens on the mechanism, not its origin — which is exactly why this page insists the roles are emergent, not designed.
Reasoned, or stumbled upon? — both Self-attention was intuition-guided empiricism, not a derivation:
  • Reasoned: attention was invented to fix a concrete bottleneck — early translation models crushed a whole sentence into one fixed vector, which broke on long inputs; letting the decoder look back at every input word was a targeted fix. The ÷√d scaling (stops big dot-products from saturating softmax) and positional encodings (needed because attention is order-blind) were also reasoned patches.
  • Stumbled/empirical: dropping recurrence entirely ("attention is all you need") was a bold bet, validated by experiments and ablations — the authors have said they were surprised how well it worked. Head counts and sinusoidal-vs-learned position were tuned empirically. The clean Q/K/V story was a post-hoc abstraction borrowed from retrieval.
The lesson: deep-learning ideas are usually engineered against bottlenecks and kept because they scale, then explained cleanly afterwards.

9Why Q and K are not interchangeable

The deepest question on this page: if the score is s = Q·K and multiplication is commutative, what stops query and key from being the same thing? Four honest layers.

Layer 0 — you're right about the multiply For a single pair, Q·K = K·Q. The dot product is symmetric. So the distinction between query and key cannot come from the multiplication itself. Good — hold onto that. The answer is that the multiply sits inside an asymmetric machine.
Layer 1 — swapping the matrices transposes the scores Swap the whole matrices, W_Q ↔ W_K. Then every score flips its indices: the new S is the transpose of the old one (S → Sᵀ), because Q_i·K_j becomes K_i·Q_j = (old S)_{ji}. So the two matrices are interchangeable only if the attention pattern you want is symmetric (S = Sᵀ). Language is full of directional relations — a pronoun points back to its antecedent, a verb to its subject, a word to the one before it — none of which are mutual. Directionality alone already forbids W_Q = W_K.
Layer 1 in full — the transpose, with indices Write everything with indices. With Q = X·W_Q and K = X·W_K, the score matrix is
Sij = Qi · Kj = Σd Qi,d Kj,d
Read the matrix by axis: row i of S is "token i as a query, scanning every key"; column j is "token j as a key, scanned by every query." Attention of token i = softmax across its row.

Now swap the two matrices — set W_Q' = W_K, W_K' = W_Q. Then Q'i = XiW_K = Ki and K'j = XjW_Q = Qj, so
S'ij = Q'i·K'j = Ki·Qj = Qj·Ki = Sji  ⟹  S' = Sᵀ
Swapping the weight matrices transposes the whole score matrix — it turns every "i looks at j" into "j looks at i."

The point in one line: unless S = Sᵀ (a symmetric attention pattern), the transpose is a different matrix, so swapping Q and K computes a different — wrong — set of attentions. Directional tasks need S ≠ Sᵀ, which is exactly when the two matrices are pinned to distinct, non-interchangeable roles. And because row i is trained by token i's error while column j is trained by token j's error, the same index asymmetry that transposes S is what hands Q and K different gradients (Section 9, Layer 2).
Layer 2 — the asymmetric machine (the real answer) The dot product is wrapped in two operations that treat query and key differently:
  • (a) softmax runs over keys, per query. Each query row becomes its own probability distribution over the keys. Rows get normalized; columns do not. Query indexes which distribution; key competes within it.
  • (b) the value is attached to the key's position: O_i = Σ_j A_ij V_j. The content that flows out belongs to the key/value token j. So key and value are the source side; query is the destination side.
Put (a) and (b) together and you get the crux ↓
Layer 2, the crux — Q and K are wired into different outputs
click a token — see where its Q and K plug in

Q_A = one row

appears only in token A's own row → shapes only O_A (A's own output). "What do I gather for myself?"

K_A = one column

appears in every token's row → shapes every other O_i (how findable A is). "How do others match me?"

V_A = a column too

delivered to everyone who attends A → source-side content. "What do I hand over when found?"

Token A's query is used once (its own row); its key is used n times (its whole column, read by everyone). Same multiply, opposite wiring.

…so their gradients come from different places Push the loss backward and the wiring shows up as different sums:
∂L/∂Q_A = Σj (∂L/∂SAj)·Kj  ←  A's own row; blame from O_A only

∂L/∂K_A = Σi (∂L/∂SiA)·Qi  ←  the whole column; blame from every O_i
Q_A's gradient sums over its own row and traces to a single output. K_A's gradient sums over its entire column and aggregates blame from every token. Different sources of blame → different corrections → different learned functions. The multiply is symmetric; the wiring is not, and gradients follow the wiring.
The vivid version — a speed-dating night
  • Your query is your private wishlist ("someone funny"). You consult it once — to choose your own dates. Only your outcomes grade it; a bad night makes you revise your wishlist.
  • Your key is the bio pinned to your chest. Everyone reads it to decide whether to approach you. If ten people misjudge you, all ten bad interactions send corrections to your bio.
  • Your value is who you actually are on a date — graded only by the people who picked you.
Even if your wishlist and your bio held identical words today, they'd be corrected in different directions tomorrow — one judged by your experience, the other by the whole room's. That divergence of feedback is the specialization.
Layer 3 — naming vs necessity "Query = looks-for-what, key = advertises-what" are the emergent descriptions of the destination-side and source-side matrices. Which physical matrix you call "the query" is a relabelling convention (swap them and transpose every pattern → an equivalent model). That they must be different functions is forced — by the asymmetric wiring (Layer 2) plus a directional objective (Layer 1). Section P4 showed their gradients differ in value; this section is why they differ in job.

10How is position encoded?

We kept saying "K encodes which position I am." Time to justify that — and to show how real models get position, because it is not free.

In the toy, position IS the input Our tokens had one-hot embeddings — x₀ = [1,0], x₁ = [0,1]. Those are not word meanings; they are pure identity tags: [1,0] literally says "I am slot 0." Because the input is one-hot, multiplying by a matrix just selects a row, so:
Q_i = row i of W_Q     K_j = row j of W_K
i.e. W_K is a lookup table with one key vector per position. "K encodes which position I am" just means training fills those rows with a distinct label per slot. Nothing discovers position — the one-hot input hands it over, and the matrix learns a label for it.
Real models must INJECT position — it isn't free Real token embeddings carry word meaning ("cat"), which says nothing about where the word sits. And plain self-attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle the words and you get the same set of outputs — it genuinely cannot tell slot 3 from slot 10. So position has to be added on purpose:
  • Absolute sinusoidal (original Transformer): a fixed position-dependent vector is added to each embedding — PE_p[2i]=sin(p/10000^{2i/d}), PE_p[2i+1]=cos(…). Now x carries both "what word" and "which slot."
  • Rotary / relative (RoPE), used by most modern LLMs: rotate Q and K by an angle proportional to position, so Q_i·K_j depends on the distance i−j. This is how models learn "attend to the previous token" or "look 3 back."
Position as an angle on a circle The next exercise uses 2-D sinusoidal encodings: position p becomes the point [cos(pθ), sin(pθ)] — a step of angle θ around a circle. "Look one position back" then becomes, literally, "rotate my angle back by θ."

11Exercise: gradient descent, step by step

A real training loop you can run by clicking. Watch W_Q and W_K start nearly identical and specialize into distinct query vs key behaviour — on a realistic positional task.

The task — "attend to the previous token" 3 tokens with sinusoidal positions PE_p=[cos(pθ),sin(pθ)], θ=50°. Values fixed (W_V=I), so the output O = A·X. Target: each token should output the previous token's encoding — token 1→token 0, token 2→token 1, token 0→itself. That target attention is the sub-diagonal [[1,0,0],[1,0,0],[0,1,0]]. We train only W_Q, W_K from a near-identical start.

Why it forces Q≠K: with Q=K the scores are symmetric (score(p,j)=score(j,p)), so "2 attends 1" would force "1 attends 2" — but 1 must attend 0. A symmetric scorer literally cannot look backward. The objective has no choice but to pull the two matrices apart.

12Common misconceptions, answered

“So engineers decided which matrix is the query?” No. All three matrices start as near-identical random numbers. Training grinds them into different shapes; we attach the labels “query/key/value” afterwards to describe what they ended up doing. If you re-ran training with the labels swapped at the start, you’d get an equivalent model — the labels are arbitrary, the distinctness is what matters.
“If Q·K = K·Q, why don’t Q and K stay identical?” The score is symmetric, but the gradients are not. Q’s correction is (∂L/∂s)·K and K’s is (∂L/∂s)·Q — same shape, different multiplier, because Q ≠ K. Different corrections each step means they drift apart. Symmetry of the formula is not symmetry of the learning signal.
“Is V trained the same way as Q and K?” No — that’s the subtle part. V never enters the score, so it gets none of the query-key gradient. It’s corrected only through the output blend O = A·V, scaled by how much attention the word received. Q and K learn where to look; V learns what to deliver. Different circuit, different signal.
“What actually breaks the symmetry in a real model?” Both routes from P2 operate at once: real matrices are initialized with different random numbers and language is full of directional relationships (a verb points to its subject; a pronoun to its antecedent) that reward asymmetric attention. The experiment isolates the objective route to show it is sufficient on its own.
“Why keep key and value separate at all?” Because the thing you match on and the thing you deliver are different jobs. A word might be a great match for a query (its key lines up) while the content it should pass along (its value) is something else entirely. Tying them — or tying Q to K — throws away expressive power; you can watch a symmetric constraint flatten the model’s options in the P5 experiment.
“Does one head do exactly one human-nameable job?” Often not. Heads can be polysemantic — one head may participate in several relationships, and one relationship may be spread across heads. Emergence guarantees that structure forms under pressure; it does not promise that structure lines up neatly with our vocabulary. Naming a head’s job requires careful input-level probing.
